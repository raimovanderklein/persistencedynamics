# What came before

Persistence Dynamics stands on work that approached the same question from many sides: how does something keep itself going in a world that runs down? Each of these traditions named part of the answer. The theory owes each of them a specific piece.

<!-- fig:lineage -->

**Walter Cannon**, *The Wisdom of the Body* (1932), named homeostasis: the body holds its internal state steady by constant correction. Conservation, the homeostat, is that idea placed in a cycle.

**Erwin Schrödinger**, *What is Life?* (1944), framed the puzzle: a living thing keeps its order by feeding on order from its surroundings and releasing disorder back. Structure is held through movement, not stillness. That is the problem the theory sets out to answer.

**Frank Gray** (1947) described the code in which successive states differ by one bit. On two bits there is only one closed path of this kind, and it is the order of the four regimes.

**W. Ross Ashby**, *Design for a Brain* (1952), built the homeostat, a machine that finds and keeps its own stability. It gave the fourth regime type its name.

**Arthur Koestler**, *The Ghost in the Machine* (1967), coined the **holon**: everything that persists is at once a whole and a part, with a self-assertive and an integrative tendency. The persistor is a holon whose two tendencies are hold and cross, and the recursion of cycles inside cycles is Koestler's hierarchy of holons made exact.

**Ludwig von Bertalanffy**, *General System Theory* (1968), described living things as open systems that stay the same by continuously exchanging matter with their environment. Hold and cross are the two faces of that exchange.

**Ilya Prigogine**, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1977, showed that structures far from equilibrium organise themselves by dissipating energy: the flame, the convection cell, the living organism. Persistence Dynamics treats every persisting system as such a structure and asks what cycle runs inside it.

**Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela**, *Autopoiesis and Cognition* (1980), defined the living system as one that produces the components that produce it. That self-referential closure is the closure of the cycle: what Conservation releases becomes what the next Potentiality gathers.

**C. S. Holling**, from 1986, and with Lance Gunderson in *Panarchy* (2002), described the adaptive cycle in ecosystems: four phases of growth, conservation, release and reorganisation, nested across scales. It is the closest relative of the four regimes. Holling found the four phases by observation. Persistence Dynamics derives four regimes from two operations, and derives their order and the scale between levels.

What none of these traditions gave was the internal geometry of the cycle: the order its regimes must run in, the way a round's closure produces what the next round runs on, and the ratio between one level and the next. That geometry is what Persistence Dynamics adds.
